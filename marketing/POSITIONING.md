# YUUKI — Positioning & Voice

Working document. Captures the brand position, the copy that follows from
it, and the claims that are deliberately off-limits.

---

## The position: 養生 (yōjō)

"Nurturing life." A Japanese tradition of health cultivation through
moderation and consistency rather than intervention — codified in Kaibara
Ekiken's *Yōjōkun* (1713). Gentle, daily, sustained over decades.

This is the anchor. The brand is **anti-optimization**, not anti any
specific ingredient or competitor. Against hustle applied to the body.

That target is deliberately broad: it covers everyone exhausted by
12-step routines and 15-active serums, not just one trend cycle. Aiming
at a specific category we don't compete in reads as defensive rather than
principled.

**The position is true of the actual product**, which is what makes it
usable: nine ingredients, food-grade traditional actives, harsh things
deliberately rejected. The marketing follows from the formula rather than
being applied to it.

---

## Claims that stay out

Three things are off-limits. Not stylistic preferences — each one carries
real exposure.

**Named individuals as cautionary tales.** Using a living person's name
commercially requires permission (right of publicity), and framing their
health as a warning is defamation-adjacent.

**Health claims about competing product categories.** Cosmetic peptides
are not anabolic steroids — different molecules, mechanisms, delivery.
An unsubstantiated health claim about a competitor category is Lanham Act
false-advertising exposure, and competitors litigate exactly this.

**Longevity or disease claims.** A cosmetic that promises longer life
becomes a *drug* under FDA rules. That would forfeit the cosmetic
classification that currently exempts us from facility registration under
MoCRA's small-business threshold, and invites a warning letter.

**None of these are needed.** The strongest version of an anti-trend
position never names the trend. "Ingredients with a thousand-year track
record" implies everything about the alternative and lets the reader
finish the thought. Naming it sounds defensive; the restraint *is* the
brand.

## Taglines

> Nothing that needs a break.

Preferred. Concrete, and "things you cycle on and off" describes the
alternative precisely without naming it.

> Slowly, and for a long time.

> Care, not correction.

## Manifesto

For the About page or a launch post.

> There's an industry right now built on doing more to your body, faster.
> Stronger actives. Bigger stacks. Compounds nobody was using ten years
> ago, and nobody has fifty years of data on.
>
> We went the other way.
>
> Rice water. Camellia oil. Yuzu. Things people have washed their hair
> with for a thousand years — long enough that we know exactly what they
> do, which is: a little, gently, every day, forever.
>
> Nine ingredients. Nothing that stings. Nothing you cycle off. Nothing
> you need to take a break from.
>
> The goal was never to transform your hair in two weeks. It's to still
> be doing this in twenty years.

## Content: the formulation decisions are the marketing

The curly-hair community is the most ingredient-literate audience in
beauty — they read INCI lists for recreation. Content that reads as
marketing elsewhere reads as substance to them.

Most small beauty brands white-label and therefore *cannot* explain their
ingredient choices. We can. That's the unfair advantage.

Each of these is a post:

- Why sea salt came out — it lifts the cuticle, the opposite of what
  curls need. Finished a repositioning the brand had already started.
- Why steam-distilled yuzu, not cold-pressed — cold-pressed citrus peel
  carries furanocoumarins, which are phototoxic on a leave-on product.
- Why a water-based leave-in *must* be preserved, and why
  "preservative-free" is a red flag rather than a selling point.
- How a preservative was chosen — the trade-off between benzyl alcohol's
  proven broad-spectrum efficacy and its sensitisation profile, and why
  under-preserving a daily leave-on is the worse risk.
- What each of the nine ingredients is actually doing.

## Visual direction

**Not the mass-market beauty aesthetic.** That grammar is
*transformation* — one dramatic reveal, before → after. Ours is
*repetition*: the same small act, many times, over years. A brand about
slowness whose advertising promises speed is incoherent.

Reference set is Aesop, Davines, Buly — natural window light, matte
surfaces, linen and stone and ceramic, real air-dried hair, negative
space, muted palette. Restraint signals the same thing the formula does.

That aesthetic is also dramatically cheaper to shoot well. The glossy
look punishes a low budget; still-life rewards it.

Palette comes from `styles.css` — `--rice #F3EEE2`, `--kraft #D9CDB4`,
`--sumi #2A2521`, `--camellia #9C2B3B`. Match physical props (linen,
ceramic) to those values and the footage matches the site for free.

## Launch economics

**Lead with the Ritual Set, not singles.** Roughly the same fulfillment
labor, ~3.6× the contribution:

| | Single ($32) | Ritual Set ($94) |
|---|---|---|
| Materials + packaging | ~$3.50 | ~$10.50 |
| Shipping | ~$7 | ~$9 |
| Payment fees | ~$1.25 | ~$3 |
| Box, mailer, label | ~$1.50 | ~$3 |
| **Contribution** | **~$19** | **~$68** |

It also decides whether paid acquisition is viable at all: at $19
contribution a $15 CAC leaves nothing, at $68 a $25 CAC still nets $43.
And it matches the story — "three steps, every wash" is undermined by
selling one bottle.

**Pre-sell a limited founding batch.** Payment before production means
zero inventory risk, known batch size, and raw materials funded by
revenue that already exists. The wait is a feature: made after you order
*is* the brand. Scarcity here is real rather than manufactured — one
person hand-batching genuinely cannot make 500 units.

**Don't discount the founding batch.** Hold full price and add something
instead — numbered card, first access to the Curl Spray, free shipping.
Discounting to the people who want you most anchors the price low
permanently, and scarcity and discount are contradictory signals.

**Realistic first launch:** three months of consistent posting might
reach 500–1,500 followers → 5–10% join the waitlist → 10–25% of an
engaged pre-launch list buys. Roughly **15–35 sets, $1,500–3,000**. That
is the correct outcome for a founding batch: it funds challenge testing
and proves demand before committing to a manufacturer.
